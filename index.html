00Abstract

Smart Partner is an AI learning agent for EAP (English for Academic Purposes) at Xi'an Jiaotong-Liverpool University. This study asks whether correcting errors with it beats ordinary independent revision.

Thirty-six Year 1 students corrected a standard argumentative paragraph containing eight embedded grammar and vocabulary errors, eighteen working with the agent and eighteen without. They then took an unseen transfer test of the same error types, and every response was scored against a fixed answer key.

The groups did not differ significantly on the correction task (12.67 vs 11.94 out of 16; t(34) = 1.245, p = .222, d = 0.42) or on transfer (7.94 vs 7.67 out of 10; p = .463, d = 0.25). Time on task was equal and neither group introduced more new errors.

Correction success did not predict transfer success (r = .075). Self-rated confidence rose by 1.31 points in both conditions. Every comparison points the same way, towards Smart Partner, yet with 18 participants per group the study can only detect large effects, and scores near the ceiling of both scales leave little room for differences to show. A powered replication with harder materials and a delayed transfer test is planned.

01Introduction

Background and rationale

Smart Partner was built for Year 1 students at Xi'an Jiaotong-Liverpool University. It drills grammar, comments on writing, revisits vocabulary and runs mock tests, all of it pointed at the EAP exam. Most students in the cohort already use generative AI for English work in some form, and more will once the exam gets closer.

Whether the tools help is a separate question.

Most evaluations stop at the rating form filled in after a session. A rating says how the session felt. It cannot separate the effect of the tool from the effect of half an hour of practice.

So we asked for evidence of another kind. Thirty-six Year 1 students worked on the same paragraph, which carried eight planted errors. Half revised with Smart Partner open; half worked from their own knowledge. Everything they produced was scored against a fixed answer key, first on that paragraph and then on five unseen sentences of the same error types, completed without AI.

That arrangement tests two things at once: whether the agent helps with the task in front of you, and whether it leaves anything behind.

02Research questions

AI-assisted correction vs independent study

"Smart Partner" is an AI learning agent for Year 1 students at Xi'an Jiaotong-Liverpool University, offering targeted grammar practice, writing feedback, vocabulary consolidation and mock tests, with the goal of helping students pass their EAP (English for Academic Purposes) exams.

The question we put to it is the one both a product team and an examiner would ask: does correcting errors with Smart Partner produce better results than revising independently, on the task itself and on unseen material afterwards? Participants completed a real correction task in one of the two conditions, and their work was scored against a fixed answer key.

Research questions
  1. Correction accuracy — Does working with Smart Partner lead to more target errors correctly fixed in a standard EAP paragraph than independent revision?
  2. Transfer to unseen items — Does that advantage, if any, carry over to new sentences of the same error types completed afterwards without AI?
  3. Cost of learning — What does each condition cost in time on task and mental effort, and does either condition introduce more new errors?
  4. Accuracy → transfer link — Does success on the correction task predict success on the unseen transfer items?
  5. Perception vs performance — How do learners' confidence, effort and difficulty ratings track the objective scores in each condition?
03Evaluation design

A two-group controlled comparison

The design is between-subjects with a single factor: learning condition, Smart Partner or independent study, 18 participants each.

Same paragraph, same answer key, same clock. Any difference in scores traces to the resource, not the material.

Both groups worked on the identical correction task and were scored against the same fixed answer key. Confidence was also measured before and after the task, which adds a small repeated-measures element on the subjective side.

Between-subjects factor (Learning condition) — participants worked in one condition only, avoiding order effects and demand characteristics from experiencing both.
Stratifying variable (proficiency level) — low, pre-intermediate, intermediate and advanced levels were used to balance allocation; the final assignment came out exactly balanced (3 / 5 / 6 / 4 per level in each group).
Objective scoring — every target error is scored 0 / 1 / 2 (not corrected / partially / fully corrected) against a fixed answer key, giving Corrected_Total (0–16) and Transfer_Total (0–10) with no rater judgement involved.

Allocation, equivalence and scoring
  1. Stratified randomisation — allocated within level strata (low, pre-intermediate, intermediate, advanced); the realised design is perfectly balanced, χ²(3) = 0.000, p = 1.000.
  2. Baseline equivalence — no group differences in pre-task confidence (p = .537), prior Smart Partner use (p = .275) or prior generative-AI use (p = .791), Mann–Whitney tests.
  3. Fixed answer key — partial-credit scoring replaces rater judgement entirely; no rater blinding procedure is needed because scoring is mechanical.
  4. Held-constant materials — same paragraph, same eight embedded errors, same transfer items, same time frame for both conditions.
04The task

What participants actually did

The task is one full cycle of EAP error correction, taken from a genuine exam scenario: find and fix the grammar and vocabulary problems in a paragraph. Nothing about it was staged for the study; it is what students already do while preparing for the exam. The correction task took 10.8 minutes on average (range 9.3–12.0), and nobody dropped out at any stage.

Stage 1 · Pre-task ratings
  1. Report current level (low / pre-intermediate / intermediate / advanced, used for stratification) and prior use of Smart Partner and generative AI for English learning (1–4).
  2. Rate confidence in correcting grammar and vocabulary errors in an EAP paragraph (1–10).
Stage 2 · Timed correction task
  1. Receive the standard EAP paragraph with eight pre-inserted target errors (grammar and vocabulary), identical for both conditions.
  2. Correct as many errors as possible: the Smart Partner group works with the agent's grammar feedback; the independent group revises from their own knowledge, with no AI assistance.
  3. Each error is scored 0 / 1 / 2 against the answer key → Corrected_Total (0–16); submission time is recorded, and any new error introduced during revision is flagged.
Stage 3 · Transfer test and post-task ratings
  1. Unseen transfer test — five new items covering the same error types in fresh sentences, no AI available, scored 0–10. This tests not "was this paragraph fixed" but "does the correction skill carry to unseen material".
  2. Post-task ratings — mental effort (1–9), perceived difficulty (1–7), post-task confidence (1–10), plus two open questions on what was hardest and what helped.
Task materials

Standard paragraph (fixed)

One argumentative EAP paragraph (150–180 words) with 8 pre-inserted target errors spanning grammar and vocabulary, the same for every participant. The five transfer items probe the same error types in unseen sentences.

Scoring

Partial credit per error (0 = not corrected, 1 = partially corrected, 2 = fully corrected) against a fixed answer key: correction 0–16, transfer 0–10. A parallel binary code flags whether the revision introduced at least one new error.

05Conditions

The two learning conditions

The intervention is simply the resource available while correcting. Same paragraph, same eight errors, same time budget; what differs is whether Smart Partner is there or not. This maps straight onto the product decision: does working with the agent measurably beat revising on your own?

Group 1 · n = 18

Smart Partner

Participants worked through the paragraph with Smart Partner's grammar-feedback practice, using its corrections and explanations to identify and fix the eight target errors.

Prior profile (baseline) Mean prior Smart Partner use 1.78 / 4 ("once or twice"); mean prior generative-AI use 2.11 / 4. Neither differs between groups (p = .275 / .791), so familiarity with the tool cannot explain any outcome difference.

Group 2 · n = 18

Independent study

Participants corrected the identical paragraph from their own knowledge within the same time frame, with no AI tool or agent available at any point during the task.

Baseline check Pre-task confidence 5.33 vs 5.67 in the Smart Partner group (p = .537) — the two groups started from statistically indistinguishable positions, as did their EAP-level composition.
Design integrity check Randomisation landed exactly as planned: the low, pre-intermediate, intermediate and advanced levels contribute 3 / 5 / 6 / 4 participants to each group, χ²(3) = 0.000, p = 1.000, and every baseline check came back non-significant. Whatever differences appear in the outcomes, they cannot be traced to pre-existing imbalance.
06Measures

What we measure, and how

Measures fall into three tiers: primary outcomes are the objective accuracy of what the learner corrected, process measures record what the learner actually did during revision, and secondary outcomes are the subjective ratings collected before and after the task. Only the first tier can answer "did it work"; the second explains how; the third describes how it felt.

With 18 participants per group, the study is powered only for large effects. The observed advantages for Smart Partner (d = 0.42 correction, d = 0.25 transfer) sit below that bar, so a larger sample would be needed before calling them real. Score distributions also clustered near the top of both scales (correction mode = 14/16; transfer mode = 8/10), and ceiling effects compress exactly the room in which differences would appear.

How to read this The direction is consistent: the Smart Partner group scored higher on both tasks, spent the same time, and introduced no more new errors. None of it reaches significance. Correction success also does not predict transfer success (r = .075); getting the paragraph right did not mean carrying the skill to unseen items. And confidence rose from 5.50 to 6.81 regardless of condition, so subjective improvement ran ahead of anything we could measure. Promising direction, unproven effect.

09Discussion

Interpreting the findings

The headline result is a null result with a consistent direction. The Smart Partner group scored higher on correction accuracy (+0.73) and on transfer (+0.28), spent the same time (10.7 vs 10.9 min), and produced no more new errors (4 vs 2, itself non-significant, Fisher p = .658).

None of these differences approaches significance.

With 18 participants per group the design detects only large effects, roughly d ≥ 0.9 at conventional power, while the observed effects sit at d = 0.42 and 0.25. The fairest reading is that there may be a small benefit here; what the data prove is only the absence of harm.

What the scores say about learning is more interesting than the group comparison itself. Correction success on the practiced paragraph did not predict performance on the unseen items (r = .075, p = .664). Participants who fixed all eight target errors were no more likely to handle the same error types in fresh sentences.

This echoes the distinction feedback researchers draw between performance on a practiced task and durable skill (Ellis et al., 2006; Hattie & Timperley, 2007), and it cuts across both conditions: neither group showed the link. Correcting a paragraph, with or without an agent, seems to be a different skill from generalising the rules behind the corrections.

The subjective data add a cautionary layer. Confidence rose by 1.31 points on average (5.50 → 6.81) in both conditions, and neither effort (5.7 vs 5.6 of 9) nor perceived difficulty (4.5 vs 4.1 of 7) separated the AI experience from independent study.

Learners felt better after the task regardless of what had changed, which is why the study anchored its claims in scored work rather than ratings.

Product implication For Smart Partner, the short-term verdict is safe to recommend, unproven to advertise. It matched independent revision on every measure while pointing consistently in its favour. Whether it teaches transferable correction skill is still open, and the near-zero correction–transfer correlation points to the next design priority: feedback that prompts generalisation, not just correction.
10Reflection

What this study can and cannot carry

Methodological limitations The sample is one of convenience (36 students, mostly at pre-intermediate and intermediate level), so results do not generalise to the whole Year 1 cohort. With 18 per group, only large effects are detectable, and the observed d values (0.25–0.42) fall below that bar; the non-significant results here are not evidence of no effect. Scores sat near the ceiling of both scales (77–78% of maximum), which compresses the room to find differences. The transfer test was also immediate: it says nothing about retention a week or a semester later.
What the results already show
  1. A consistent direction, no proof — Smart Partner led on both outcomes with equal time and no extra new errors, but every test was non-significant; the design was honest, the sample was not large enough.
  2. Skill does not follow score — correction success and transfer success are essentially unrelated (r = .075): performing well on the paragraph did not guarantee carrying the skill to unseen items.
  3. Confidence is cheap — self-rated confidence rose +1.31 points on average in both conditions, while objective evidence of improvement remained unproven — a live demonstration of why satisfaction is not learning.
  4. The design held — perfect EAP-level balance and non-significant baseline checks mean the comparison was fair, so a larger run of the same protocol is justified.
What we would change next time
  1. Increase the sample — the observed effects would require roughly 100+ participants per group for adequate power at d ≈ 0.4.
  2. Use harder materials — a more demanding paragraph (or more errors) removes the ceiling that compressed both score distributions.
  3. Add a delayed transfer test (1 week later) to separate immediate carry-over from retention.
  4. Code errors by category (agreement, articles, word choice, …) to identify which error types benefit most from AI support.
  5. Analyse the open answers — the two open questionnaire items and the interviews are collected but not yet thematically coded (Braun & Clarke).
Research ethics

All participants read the information sheet and ticked consent before the task began; scores and ratings are stored under codes (P01–P36) with no names or student IDs; participants could withdraw at any time without affecting their grades; no condition creates academic disadvantage — the Smart Partner group received no answer key advantages unavailable to the other group after the task, and debriefing covered the study's purpose. Results are reported in aggregate only and are not traceable to individuals.

11Conclusion

What this study concludes

The question was practical: does correcting EAP errors with Smart Partner outperform ordinary independent revision? Thirty-six Year 1 students, randomly assigned in perfect balance across four proficiency levels, corrected an identical paragraph, then the same error types on unseen items. Every response was scored against a fixed answer key.

Smart Partner matched independent study everywhere and led it on both primary scores, yet nothing reached significance. The observed advantages (d = 0.42 correction, d = 0.25 transfer) would take roughly 100+ participants per group to verify.

The data show that Smart Partner does no harm. They do not show that it teaches.

Success on the practiced paragraph did not carry to unseen items in either condition (r = .075). That is the clearest finding here, and it is a design signal for the agent as much as for the research.

Confidence rose equally in both conditions while the objective evidence stayed unproven. Task-based, objectively scored evaluation should come before satisfaction-based claims.

The immediate next steps are set out in Section 10: a powered replication with harder materials, a delayed transfer test to separate carry-over from retention, error-category coding, and thematic analysis of the open-ended answers and interviews already collected.
