01Research questions

We measure whether drafts improve, not whether users approve

"Smart Partner" is an AI learning agent for Year 1 students at Xi'an Jiaotong-Liverpool University, offering targeted grammar practice, writing feedback, vocabulary consolidation and mock tests, with the goal of helping students pass their EAP (English for Academic Purposes) exams. Evaluation of such tools usually stops at "did you find it useful" — but satisfaction is not learning: students may rate highly a tool that silently fixes their errors without making them think. This study therefore asks participants to complete a real learning task first, and judges effectiveness by what their work looks like afterwards.

Why a questionnaire alone is not an evaluation

Using questionnaires as primary evidence has four irreparable flaws: (i) no objective anchor — "do you feel you improved" cannot be checked against "how much your draft actually improved"; (ii) effects cannot be attributed — reported gains may come from practice itself or the passage of time, not from the AI's feedback style; (iii) stated wants diverge from effective design — the feature students say they most want is often not the one that drives revision; (iv) recall bias — self-reported frequency and duration are systematically distorted. Our response: the questionnaire is demoted to a post-task supplementary instrument, used to explain the objective results (why one condition produced larger gains or heavier load), never as the evidence of effectiveness itself.

Research questions
  1. Between-group main effect — Does revised draft quality differ between participants receiving direct correction and guided scaffolding?
  2. Within-group main effect — Regardless of condition, does draft quality improve significantly from first draft to revision?
  3. Interaction (core) — Does feedback style moderate the size of the gain, i.e. is there a feedback × time interaction?
  4. Process mechanism — Do revision behaviours (turns, adoption rate, self-corrections) predict the size of the gain?
  5. Correspondence check — Do subjective ratings (usefulness, trust, cognitive load) agree with objective revision quality?
02Evaluation design

A 2 × 2 mixed design

This study uses a mixed (split-plot) factorial design containing one between-subjects factor and one within-subjects factor. The advantage is threefold: we can compare the relative merit of two AI feedback styles, confirm whether each participant individually improved, and test the question with the greatest design value — which style produces the larger gain.

Between-subjects factor (Feedback style) — participants are randomised to A (direct correction) or B (guided scaffolding) and receive only one, avoiding order effects and demand characteristics from experiencing both.
Within-subjects factor (Time) — all participants are measured at T1 (first draft) and T2 (final draft), so each person serves as their own control, cancelling out individual writing proficiency, the largest confound.
Stratifying variable (EAP level) — A / B / C / High Level is used to stratify randomisation so that the two conditions stay balanced across levels.

Randomisation and blinding
  1. Stratified block randomisation — stratify by EAP level, randomise A/B within stratum using blocks of 4, with the sequence generated by a member not involved in data collection.
  2. Rater blinding — final drafts are stripped of condition labels and interaction logs before scoring; two raters score independently without knowing the condition.
  3. Inter-rater reliability — assessed with ICC(2,2), target > 0.75; disagreements are adjudicated by a third rater.
  4. Held-constant materials — all participants use the same prompt, the same time limit and the same scoring rubric; only the feedback style differs.
03The task

What participants actually do

The task is one complete cycle of EAP paragraph writing and revision, lasting about 35 minutes and run continuously in three stages. It is drawn from a genuine EAP practice scenario — write an argumentative paragraph, then revise it in response to feedback — so what participants do is what they would normally do while preparing for the exam, rather than a performance staged for the study.

Stage 1 · Baseline writing (10 min, no AI)
  1. Read the fixed writing prompt (argumentative; see materials below).
  2. Independently write a 150–180-word argumentative paragraph within 10 minutes, without any AI tool or dictionary.
  3. Submit the first draft and immediately complete the pre-test self-ratings (confidence 1–10, perceived task difficulty).
Stage 2 · AI-assisted revision (15 min)
  1. Following the pre-generated randomisation sequence, the researcher opens a Smart Partner session in the participant's assigned condition.
  2. The participant submits their first draft to Smart Partner for feedback and revises accordingly. The entire interaction is logged: turns, duration, items of AI advice, items actually adopted into the final draft, and errors the participant detected and fixed unaided.
  3. The researcher does not intervene or prompt, and simply collects the final draft when time expires.
Stage 3 · Post-test and delayed transfer
  1. Immediate post-test (5 min) — after submitting the final draft, complete perceived usefulness, trust, cognitive load and post-test confidence.
  2. Delayed transfer test (1 week later, 10 min) — write another paragraph on a new prompt of the same type, with no AI available. This stage tests not "was this draft fixed" but "does the learning persist" — the outcome on which the two feedback styles are expected to diverge.
Task materials

Writing prompt (fixed)

"Some people argue that universities should allow students to use AI tools in all assessed work. To what extent do you agree or disagree?"
Requirements: 150–180 words, one clear position and at least one supporting reason.

Scoring rubric

EAP paragraph criteria: language accuracy (0–40), argument structure and cohesion (0–30), appropriateness of academic register (0–30); total 0–100. Two trained raters score independently and the mean is used.

04Intervention

The two feedback styles

The intervention is simply how Smart Partner replies to the draft. Both conditions flag the same set of errors; the only difference is whether the correct form is supplied. This contrast maps directly onto a product decision: should the AI fix things for the user, or make the user work it out?

Condition A

Direct correction

Supplies the corrected text along with an itemised list of error types and correct forms. The participant can complete the revision by simply applying them.

Sample reply "Sentence 2 has a subject–verb agreement error: use The results suggest, not The results suggests. In sentence 4, however is misused; replace it with whereas to signal contrast. Here is the fully revised paragraph: …"

Condition B

Guided scaffolding

Flags only the location and type of each error and prompts the participant to correct it, without supplying the correct form.

Sample reply "Sentence 2 has a subject–verb agreement problem — the subject is plural, so check the verb. In sentence 4 a linker is misused: you mean to signal contrast, so is the word you chose right for that? Send it back when you have revised."
Manipulation check Two items confirm the intervention landed: (i) "Did the AI give you the correct wording directly?" (yes/no); (ii) "How much did you have to think for yourself while revising?" (1–7). If most of condition A answer "no" to (i), or the two conditions do not differ on (ii), the manipulation is treated as failed and the run is repeated.
05Measures

What we measure, and how

Measures fall into three tiers: primary outcomes are the objective quality of what the learner produced, process measures record what the learner actually did, and secondary outcomes are subjective ratings collected after the task. Only the first tier can answer "did it work"; the second explains how; the third describes how it felt.

How to read this If condition A turns out better on the immediate revision while condition B is better on transfer, that indicates a classic performance–learning trade-off: direct correction makes people "look better now", guided scaffolding makes them "actually better later". No satisfaction questionnaire could reach this conclusion; it requires having participants complete a real task and measuring how their output changes.

08Reflection

What this design cannot carry

Methodological limitations The sample is one of convenience, likely concentrated in the two core groups B and C and thin in A and High Level; stratified randomisation balances condition assignment, but the sample is not representative of the Year 1 cohort. A single 35-minute session covers one writing–revision cycle only, so nothing can be said about decay or accumulation over sustained use. The transfer test sits just one week out — evidence of short-term retention, not of learning at the scale of a semester.
What we expect to go wrong
  1. Condition contamination — participants in the same class may compare AI replies and notice two conditions exist. Sessions will be scheduled apart and the design debriefed only after the task.
  2. Unequal revision effort — condition B is harder, so participants may give up early. Time of disengagement will be logged and checked as a covariate.
  3. Rater drift — standards may loosen over a long scoring session. Order will be randomised and agreement re-checked every 20 scripts.
  4. Attrition at transfer — some participants will not return after a week. Two-session commitment is stated at recruitment, and missing data will be handled with a sensitivity analysis.
What we would change next time
  1. Switch to stratified sampling with quotas across the four EAP levels, at least 15 per cell per level.
  2. Add a later follow-up (1 week + 1 month) to separate short-term retention from long-term transfer.
  3. Add a no-AI control arm to separate the effect of AI feedback from the effect of simply rewriting once.
  4. Code errors by category (agreement, articles, cohesion, etc.) to identify which error types benefit most from guided feedback.
Research ethics

All participants read the information sheet and tick consent before the task begins; drafts and interaction logs are stored under codes with no names or student IDs; participants may withdraw at any time without affecting their grades; neither condition creates academic disadvantage — participants in condition B receive a full explanation once the task ends, so no knowledge gap is left behind. Results are reported in aggregate only and are not traceable to individuals.
